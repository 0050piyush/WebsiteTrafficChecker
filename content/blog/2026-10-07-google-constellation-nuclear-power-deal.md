Google has signed one of the largest corporate power deals in the US. On Tuesday, October 6, the company and Constellation Energy announced agreements covering 3,590 megawatts on PJM, the grid that serves Washington, D.C., and all or part of 13 states from Illinois to New Jersey. That's roughly the output of three large nuclear reactors.

The number that got the most attention is smaller: 890 MW of nuclear capacity that doesn't exist yet. Constellation will create it without building a single new reactor.

## Two deals in one

The agreement has two parts.

The first is a 20-year power purchase agreement for 890 MW of new nuclear output. Constellation will get it by upgrading 11 reactors it already operates at six plants in Illinois, Pennsylvania and New Jersey. The industry calls these projects **uprates**: new or more efficient turbines, replacement steam generators and modern digital control systems that let an existing unit produce more electricity. Constellation puts the investment at more than $4.3 billion, according to the [American Nuclear Society](https://www.ans.org/news/article-8467/google-constellation-reach-11unit-uprate-plan-constellation-to-invest-43-billion/). The first upgraded unit should start delivering extra power in 2028, and the full 890 MW is due by the end of 2032.

The second part is a 15-year supply agreement for another 2,700 MW across PJM. New nuclear capacity makes up about a quarter of the total, as [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/06/google-enters-massive-3point6-gw-power-deal-with-constellation-energy-.html) put it.

## Why upgrade old reactors instead of building new ones

Speed and certainty. The last new reactors built in the US, Vogtle units 3 and 4 in Georgia, came online in 2023 and 2024, years behind schedule and billions over budget. An uprate is still a multi-year engineering project, and each one needs approval from the Nuclear Regulatory Commission, but it works on equipment that's already licensed, staffed and connected to the grid.

That last point matters in PJM. Getting a new power plant connected there means waiting in a long interconnection queue, and the backlog has become one of the biggest constraints on how quickly the grid can add supply. Capacity squeezed out of existing plants largely skips that wait.

For Google, the attraction is power that runs around the clock without carbon emissions. Data centers serving AI and search traffic draw power day and night, and the company has a goal of matching its consumption with carbon-free energy every hour of every day by 2030. Wind and solar can't do that alone without a lot of storage.

For Constellation, a 20-year contract with one of the world's most valuable companies turns a $4.3 billion capital plan into a much safer bet. Investors read it that way: the stock closed up about 12% on the day of the announcement.

## Part of a pattern

Constellation has become the go-to supplier for tech companies that want nuclear power. Microsoft signed with it in 2024 to restart a reactor at Three Mile Island in Pennsylvania, now renamed the Crane Clean Energy Center. Meta followed in 2025 with a 20-year agreement tied to Constellation's Clinton plant in Illinois. Google has also backed small modular reactors from Kairos Power, though those aren't expected to supply power at scale until the 2030s.

What's different here is the approach. Rather than reviving one plant or waiting on new designs, the deal spreads modest upgrades across 11 units, with the first megawatts arriving in 2028.

## What it means beyond the power sector

The cost of running AI is turning into a question of electricity as much as chips. Companies that can sign 20-year contracts are locking in supply now, while capacity prices in PJM have climbed with data-center demand. Smaller cloud providers, and the businesses that rent from them, don't get deals like this. They pay what the market charges.

If you run a website or an online business, the effect is indirect but real. The price and availability of cloud computing, and of the AI features built on it, increasingly depend on who has secured power. Google just secured a lot of it.

## What to watch

- **Regulatory approvals.** Each uprate needs NRC sign-off before the extra output can flow.
- **The 2028 milestone.** The first upgraded unit is the test of whether the schedule holds.
- **More deals of this shape.** With Microsoft, Meta and now Google all buying from Constellation, other large nuclear operators are likely to see similar offers from data-center builders.
