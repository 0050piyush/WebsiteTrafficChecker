AMD has been raising its chip supply all year, and it still can't make enough. That was the message from CEO Lisa Su during a one-day visit to Taiwan on Tuesday, October 6, where she met five of the company's key partners: TSMC, Foxconn, Quanta, Asus and Acer.

"We've been able to increase our supply as we've gone through 2026, and we're going to substantially increase our supply in 2027, but we can definitely use more," Su said, as reported by [Focus Taiwan](https://focustaiwan.tw/business/202610060011) and [Investing.com](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/amd-plans-to-boost-chip-supply-in-2027-to-meet-ai-demand-93CH-4933657).

Investors liked what they heard. AMD shares rose 2.8% on the day, according to [Zacks' daily company roundup](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/company-news-oct-7-2026-075100947.html).

## Planning five years out

The most telling detail wasn't the 2027 promise. It was how far ahead AMD now has to plan.

Su said capacity planning that used to look one to two years ahead now stretches three, four or even five years, [according to TrendForce](https://www.trendforce.com/news/2026/10/06/news-amds-lisa-su-visits-tsmc-ai-supply-chain-partners-as-company-locks-in-capacity-3-5-years-ahead/). Wafer production, packaging and the substrates chips are mounted on all have to be ready at the same time, and each step has its own suppliers and lead times. When customers commit to data centers that take years to build, chipmakers have to lock in capacity just as early.

Su told reporters she expects demand to stay "very, very high" for the next several years, [Benzinga reported](https://www.benzinga.com/markets/prediction-markets/26/10/62190764/amd-ai-demand-lisa-su-supply).

## More money for Taiwan

In May, AMD announced a $10 billion investment in its Taiwan supply chain. Su said that plan is on track, and that AMD will now go beyond it because demand has kept growing. She didn't say by how much.

The visit list explains where that money goes. TSMC makes AMD's most advanced processors and AI accelerators. Foxconn and Quanta build the servers and racks they go into. Asus and Acer turn AMD's chips into PCs and laptops. A shortage at any one of them caps how many products AMD can ship.

## Why supply is the bottleneck

For most of the AI boom, the limit on AI hardware hasn't been demand. It's been how many advanced chips the industry can physically produce and package. Nvidia, AMD and the large cloud companies building their own chips all compete for the same leading-edge manufacturing and advanced packaging capacity, much of it at TSMC.

AMD's biggest customers are committing years ahead. The best-known example is OpenAI's 2025 agreement to deploy six gigawatts of AMD GPUs over several years, starting in the second half of 2026. Deals of that size only work if the supply chain behind them is booked well in advance, which is exactly what Su was in Taiwan to do.

## What it means

- **For cloud and AI customers:** more AMD supply in 2027 should mean more choice in AI hardware and more pressure on prices, though Su's own comments suggest shortages won't disappear.
- **For Nvidia:** it remains the market leader, but a competitor with more chips to sell is a competitor that can win more large contracts.
- **For everyone else:** the cost of AI features in the apps and services you use comes down, eventually, to how much computing power exists. Announcements like this one are an early signal of where that supply is heading.

## What to watch

- AMD's next quarterly results, for any update on 2027 capacity and the size of the expanded Taiwan investment.
- TSMC's capacity plans for advanced packaging, which have been a bottleneck for the whole industry.
- Whether other chipmakers follow AMD in talking about planning horizons of five years rather than two.
